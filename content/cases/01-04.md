title: "Case 1 · Food delivery (Swiggy/Zomato-style): scheduled orders and checkout conversion"
category: "Marketplace|Conversion|Discovery"
order: 1
summary: Should a food-delivery app push scheduled orders, and how do we lift checkout conversion without hurting trust?
---

!!! hypo "Hypothetical scenario"
    Swiggy and Zomato are large Indian food-delivery platforms; this case uses that *type* of product. The problem framing, evidence and every number below are **invented for teaching** — they are not data about either company.

**Business background.** A food-delivery marketplace sees demand spikes at lunch and dinner. Peak spikes strain courier supply and raise delivery times; off-peak capacity sits idle.

**Target users.** Office workers ordering lunch; families ordering dinner; restaurants; delivery partners.

**Customer problem.** Customers who know *when* they want food (e.g., 1 pm lunch) still order at the last minute and face long ETAs at peak; many abandon at checkout when fees and ETA appear.

**Business objective.** Raise completed orders and on-time rate at peak while improving checkout conversion.

**Available evidence (hypothetical).**

| Signal | Value |
|---|---|
| Checkout completion (peak) | 64% vs 72% off-peak |
| Share of abandoners who viewed ETA > 40 min | 38% |
| Interviews (10 office workers) | 7 plan lunch before 11 am; 6 said they'd schedule if reliable |
| Existing scheduled-order share | 3% of orders (feature hidden in a menu) |

**Assumptions to test.** (1) Customers will schedule if the slot is guaranteed; (2) restaurants can prepare to a slot; (3) smoothing demand improves on-time rate; (4) showing fee + ETA earlier reduces abandonment.

**Product hypothesis.** *Making scheduling visible in the cart for peak-hour orders, with a guaranteed slot, will raise peak checkout completion by ≥ 3 points and on-time delivery by ≥ 5 points, without reducing order volume.*

```chart
type: steps
title: Target journey with scheduling
steps:
  - ["Browse at 11:30", "Sees 'Schedule for 1:00 pm — on time guaranteed'"]
  - ["Cart", "Total incl. fees + slot shown early"]
  - ["Pay", "Confirmation with slot"]
  - ["Prep", "Restaurant receives order with prep time"]
  - ["Deliver", "On-time arrival; feedback"]
caption: Hypothetical target journey. The guarantee is only credible if operations can meet it — test before promising.
```

**Candidate solutions.** A) Surface scheduling in cart at peak; B) Early fee + ETA display; C) Peak pricing to shift demand; D) Office “group lunch” scheduled orders; E) Restaurant prep-time integration.

**Prioritisation (RICE, hypothetical).** B: 60,000 × 2 × 0.8 ÷ 2 = **48,000**; A: 40,000 × 2 × 0.5 ÷ 3 ≈ **13,333**; D: 8,000 × 3 × 0.5 ÷ 5 = **2,400**; C excluded as first move (trust risk). Start with **B**, run **A** as a pilot in one city.

**MVP.** Early fee/ETA display (A/B) + scheduling banner in cart for orders started 11:00–12:30 in one city with 50 partner restaurants.

**KPIs.** Primary: peak checkout completion. Secondary: on-time rate at peak, scheduled-order share. Guardrails: total orders per user, cancellations, restaurant acceptance rate, complaint rate.

**Risks and constraints.** Missed scheduled slots destroy trust; restaurant capacity; courier allocation logic; cannibalisation (no net gain, just shifting).

**Experiment plan.** 1) A/B test early fee/ETA (Module 7 sizing). 2) City pilot for scheduling with holdout zones; 4 weeks; daily ops review.

**Example metrics (hypothetical readout).** Early fee/ETA: completion 64.0% → 66.4% (+2.4 pts, CI +1.1 to +3.7). Scheduling pilot: scheduled share 3% → 11%; on-time at peak 78% → 83%; total orders per user unchanged (no cannibalisation detected).

**Recommendation.** Ship early fee/ETA; expand scheduling to two more cities with restaurant prep-time integration first.

**Potential outcomes.** Best: smoother peaks, lower costs, happier customers. Worst: unreliable slots → complaints → roll back the guarantee.

**Key PM lessons.** Fix the obvious friction (fees, ETA) before building new behaviour; operational promises need operational proof; measure cannibalisation, not just adoption.

id: ride-hailing-cancellations
title: "Case 2 · Ride-hailing (Uber/Ola-style): cancellation rates and marketplace dynamics"
category: "Marketplace|Metrics|Strategy"
order: 2
summary: Reduce ride cancellations without breaking the supply–demand balance.
---

!!! hypo "Hypothetical scenario"
    Uber and Ola are ride-hailing platforms operating in India. All framing and numbers here are **invented**; they do not describe either company's data or systems. (Your course's surge-pricing session, Lecture 15, is summarised in Module 8.)

**Business background.** A two-sided marketplace matches riders and drivers. Cancellations waste time on both sides and damage trust.

**Target users.** Riders (commuters, airport trips); drivers (full-time and part-time).

**Customer problem.** Riders wait, then the driver cancels; drivers accept, then riders cancel or are far away.

**Business objective.** Reduce total cancellation rate while keeping completed trips and driver earnings stable.

**Available evidence (hypothetical).**

```chart
type: pareto
title: Cancellation reasons (hypothetical sample of 1,000 cancellations)
items: [["Driver: pickup too far", 290], ["Rider: ETA too long", 210], ["Driver: destination not preferred", 170], ["Rider: found another ride", 140], ["Payment mode mismatch", 110], ["Other", 80]]
caption: Hypothetical. Driver-side reasons (pickup distance, destination) account for nearly half.
```

**Assumptions.** Showing destination before acceptance changes driver behaviour; better pickup-distance matching reduces driver cancellations; honest ETAs reduce rider cancellations.

**Product hypothesis.** *Capping pickup distance per trip type and showing a reliable ETA with a confidence band will cut cancellations by ≥ 15% without lowering completed trips.*

**User journey.** Request → match → accept → drive to pickup → trip → pay → rate. Cancellations cluster at accept→pickup.

**Candidate solutions.** A) Smarter matching (distance caps, batched matching); B) Destination preview for drivers (with fairness safeguards); C) Cancellation fees/penalties; D) Rider ETA confidence bands; E) Incentives for long pickups.

**Prioritisation.** Weighted scoring (impact 40%, trust 25%, feasibility 20%, cost 15%) favours **A** and **D**; **C** risks trust and fairness; **B** risks discrimination by destination → require safeguards and policy review.

**MVP.** Distance caps + ETA bands in one city, randomised by geography/time (switchback design to limit interference).

**KPIs.** Primary: cancellation rate per request. Secondary: completed trips per hour, time to pickup. Guardrails: driver earnings per online hour, rider wait time, complaint rate, fairness (cancellations by area).

**Risks.** Marketplace interference (treatment affects control through shared drivers) — use switchback or geo tests; reduced matches in sparse areas; regulatory pricing rules.

**Experiment plan.** Switchback test (alternating hourly treatment) for 3 weeks; analyse by area and time.

**Example metrics (hypothetical).** Cancellation 19.0% → 16.1% (−15%); completed trips/hour unchanged; driver earnings/online hour −1% (within guardrail); sparse areas saw longer waits → adjust caps there.

**Recommendation.** Roll out with area-specific caps; explore destination preview only with fairness controls.

**Key PM lessons.** Marketplace experiments need interference-aware designs; optimise the system, not one side; guardrails for fairness.

id: ecommerce-discovery
title: "Case 3 · Amazon-style e-commerce: discovery, conversion and recommendations"
category: "Consumer|Conversion|Personalisation"
order: 3
summary: Help shoppers find the right product faster and convert more, without eroding trust.
---

!!! hypo "Hypothetical scenario"
    Amazon is widely known for features such as product recommendations (“customers who bought this also bought”) and customer reviews. This case is a **hypothetical scenario** for a large e-commerce marketplace; numbers are invented.

**Business background.** A marketplace with millions of products; search and recommendations drive most discovery.

**Target users.** Shoppers on mobile; sellers.

**Customer problem.** Search results for vague queries (“running shoes for flat feet”) are noisy; shoppers bounce between filters and reviews.

**Business objective.** Increase search-to-purchase conversion and reduce time to purchase.

**Available evidence (hypothetical).** 41% of searches are “descriptive” (needs, not product names); their conversion is 2.1% vs 6.8% for navigational searches; session replays show repeated filter changes; review mining shows confusion about fit.

**Assumptions.** Semantic understanding of needs improves relevance; review summaries reduce decision time; personalised ranking helps without creating filter bubbles.

**Product hypothesis.** *Semantic search with need-based facets and AI-generated review summaries (with citations to reviews) will raise conversion of descriptive searches by ≥ 15% relative.*

```chart
type: bar
title: Conversion by search type (hypothetical baseline)
cats: ["Navigational ('brand X shoe 42')", "Descriptive ('shoes for flat feet')"]
series:
  "Search-to-purchase conversion (%)": [6.8, 2.1]
fmt: pct
caption: Hypothetical. The gap identifies descriptive searches as the opportunity.
```

**Candidate solutions.** A) Hybrid semantic search; B) Need-based facets (“arch support”); C) Review summaries with “what buyers say about fit”; D) Personalised ranking; E) Guided Q&A shopping assistant.

**Prioritisation.** RICE favours **A** and **C** (high reach, moderate effort); **E** has low confidence and higher risk (hallucinated product claims).

**MVP.** Hybrid search for one category (footwear) + review summaries with links to source reviews.

**KPIs.** Primary: descriptive-search conversion. Secondary: time to add-to-cart, filter changes per session. Guardrails: return rate, complaints about inaccurate summaries, seller fairness.

**Risks.** Hallucinated summaries (must ground in reviews), review manipulation, privacy in personalisation, seller ranking fairness.

**Experiment plan.** A/B by user for 3 weeks in footwear; human evaluation of 300 summaries for accuracy before launch.

**Example metrics (hypothetical).** Descriptive conversion 2.1% → 2.5% (+19% relative); returns unchanged; 1.3% of summaries flagged as inaccurate in review → fix grounding before expansion.

**Recommendation.** Expand semantic search; hold summaries until inaccuracy < 0.5% on evaluation.

**Key PM lessons.** Segment the funnel by intent; ground AI in source content; measure returns and trust, not just conversion.

title: "Case 4 · Netflix-style streaming: personalisation and retention"
category: "Consumer|Retention|Personalisation"
order: 4
summary: Reduce subscriber churn by helping members find something worth watching quickly.
---

!!! hypo "Hypothetical scenario"
    Netflix publicly describes using personalised recommendations and has written about personalising artwork on its technology blog. This case is a **hypothetical** streaming-service scenario; numbers are invented.

**Business background.** Subscription streaming; content costs are high; churn is the main threat to revenue.

**Target users.** Members on TV and mobile; households with multiple profiles.

**Customer problem.** Members spend minutes browsing without choosing (“decision fatigue”) and may conclude “there's nothing to watch,” a churn risk.

**Business objective.** Reduce monthly churn and increase hours of satisfied viewing.

**Available evidence (hypothetical).** Members who start a title within 2 minutes of opening the app churn at 2.1%/month vs 4.8% for those who often abandon sessions; surveys cite “too much choice”; 30% of sessions end without playback.

**Assumptions.** Faster time-to-play reduces churn (correlation now — needs a causal test); better rows/artwork help choice; a “continue watching” improvement matters most for returning members.

**Product hypothesis.** *Personalised row ordering plus a “quick pick” for short sessions will reduce sessions-without-play by 15% and, over 3 months, reduce churn by 0.3 points.*

```chart
type: line
title: Retention by time-to-first-play behaviour (hypothetical cohorts)
xs: ["M0", "M1", "M2", "M3", "M4", "M5", "M6"]
series:
  "Usually plays within 2 min": [100, 97.9, 95.8, 93.8, 91.9, 89.9, 88.0]
  "Often abandons sessions": [100, 95.2, 90.6, 86.3, 82.1, 78.2, 74.4]
ylabel: "% still subscribed"
ymin: 70
ymax: 100
fmt: pct
caption: Hypothetical; constant monthly churn of 2.1% vs 4.8%. Correlation, not causation — members who abandon may simply like the catalogue less.
```

**Candidate solutions.** A) Personalised row ordering; B) Quick pick / shuffle; C) Better “continue watching” management; D) Artwork personalisation; E) Content-gap acquisition (not a product fix).

**Prioritisation.** A and C (high reach, medium effort, good confidence); B as a cheap test.

**MVP.** Row-ordering model change for 10% of members; quick-pick button test.

**KPIs.** Primary: sessions-without-play rate (leading); churn (lagging, 3-month holdout). Guardrails: completion rate (are people enjoying what they start?), complaints about repetitive recommendations, diversity of genres watched.

**Risks.** Optimising clicks over satisfaction; filter bubbles; long time to measure churn.

**Experiment plan.** A/B on row ordering 6 weeks with a long-term holdout for churn.

**Example metrics (hypothetical).** Sessions-without-play 30% → 26.5% (−12%); completion rate flat; 3-month churn delta −0.2 pts (CI includes 0) — continue holdout.

**Recommendation.** Ship row ordering; keep a holdout for churn; iterate quick pick.

**Key PM lessons.** Use leading indicators with long-term holdouts; separate correlation from causation; guard against engagement-at-all-costs.
